My third SIV, and the one I had been building toward without quite realising it.

The first was at this same lake in California, three years ago. The second was in
France, over Lake Annecy, two years ago. This one was the first I have done on a
C wing and on a two-liner, which was the whole reason to book it — you do not
want the first time you find out how your wing behaves at the edge of its
envelope to be a day when you did not choose it.

It went great. I did not have to go swimming, which I am counting as the primary
success metric.

## The list

Everything was on the Nova Vortex, with MaxAcro running the course.

I focused on stalls, and went through them in order: deep stall, dynamic stall,
full stall. Then backfly, then backfly 360s. Then dynamic turns into pitch
oscillations, which is where the course stops being a list of recoveries and
starts being about rhythm — you are not reacting to the wing any more, you are
driving it.

From there I started working on the heli. I did not get one. But by the end I
could definitely feel the rotation starting to come, which is a different kind of
"not yet" than the one I showed up with.

## Why bother

An SIV is the only place you get to be wrong on purpose, over water, with someone
on the radio who has seen it a thousand times.

Everything else in this section of the site is a flight where the goal was to go
somewhere. This is the opposite: the goal is to break the wing out of flight,
repeatedly, until the recovery is boring. It is the same instinct as writing the
test for the failure path — you would rather meet it in a place you control.

The group made the weekend. A fine bunch of pilots, and a genuinely fun few days
for something that is nominally about things going wrong.

Next one is in Washington at the end of August, and it will be the first on the
GTO 3.
