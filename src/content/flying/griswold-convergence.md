The day after the Tollhouse hundred, we flew a place called Griswold. It is the
most interesting flight of the three and the one I got wrong.

## Conditions

Griswold only works a couple of times a year, which is most of what makes it
worth writing about.

When it does work, it is a downwind convergence flight. Wind comes in naturally
from the coast — a west wind — and another flow comes in from the east, and the
two meet over the centre of a mountain range that runs from east of Fresno south
toward San Luis Obispo. Where they collide, there is a line of lift you cannot
see.

A friend of ours discovered this not that long ago. That still amazes me: that
there are places like this being found now, by people flying around and paying
attention.

## The hike

It is a hike and fly. About twelve of us did the hour and a half up the front
range of a smaller mountain.

## The flight

The plan has stages, and each one is a gate you either pass or you go home.

Launch, and catch a thermal that tops out around 5,000 feet. That is all the
front range gives you, and it is what you have to commit the valley crossing on
— a long valley, with plenty of opportunities to bomb out in it and no way to
get across without accepting that. The valley is where the day ends for a lot of
people.

I made it to the back range, and the back range is a completely different
atmosphere: more like 13,000 feet once you are established on it. Nearly triple
what you left with, which tells you something about how much of this flight is
decided by whether you are willing to leave on a low climb.

The next problem is to get deeper into the mountains, away from the roads,
because that is where the convergence line actually is. Once you are on it, the
rest of the flight goes boaty — continuous, very strong, and turbulent with it.

It all worked to plan. I got deep, I got on the line, and it did what it was
supposed to do.

## Decisions I would change

Toward the end I started following the terrain.

That is the whole mistake in one sentence. I reverted to normal XC flying —
reading the ground, working the features, doing the thing that is correct almost
everywhere else — and forgot that on this day the thing to follow was the
invisible line, not the visible mountain. Normal XC strategy put me on the
ground. Safely, and immediately picked up by our ground crew, but on the ground.

The pilots who kept following the convergence line went up to 200 km.

I did fly well over 100 km, and no personal record came out of it, and neither
of those facts is the interesting part. The interesting part is that I had the
right model of the day for most of the flight and then quietly swapped it for a
more familiar one at exactly the moment it stopped applying. The line was still
there. I just stopped flying it.

Long drive back once we had collected everyone. Good conversation, which is
easier to have when the person telling the story is the one who landed first.
